The Rizal Law

Republic Act No. 1425

RA
1425
RIZAL LAW
signed 1956

Purpose & Significance

  • All schools (public and private) shall include Rizal's life, works and writings including Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo in their curricula.
  • Written to inspire Nationalism and pride of race after the long years of the Filipino people’s struggle for freedom from colonial rule.
  • Rizal's novels model how literature can expose corruption, abuse of power, and social injustice — issues Filipinos still confront.
  • Approved by Congress despite fervent disapproval by number of churches due to Rizal’s severe critiques of Catholic life in the Philippines as exposed in his novels.

Why It Remains Relevant Today

  • Rizal’s novels can serve as models for literary works that expose corruption, abuse of power and social injustices in the Philippines today.
  • Helps build critical thinking and civic consciousness.
  • Anchors a shared sense of national identity in an era of globalization and historical distortion online.
  • Reminds each generation that reform begins with education and not with mere protest.

19th-Century Philippines

The world that shaped Rizal

Political

Spain ruled through a centralized, friar-influenced colonial government. Filipinos had no real representation in the Spanish Cortes, and abuses by local officials went largely unchecked.

Social

Society was stratified by race — peninsulares, insulares, mestizos, and indios were treated unequally. Friars wielded enormous influence over education, land, and daily life.

Economic

Friar-owned haciendas (like the one leased by the Rizal family in Calamba) and colonial monopolies restricted local prosperity, while the 1869 opening of the Suez Canal sped up trade and travel to Europe.

Cultural

A rising, Europe-educated ilustrado class absorbed Enlightenment and liberal ideas, while the execution of Gomburza in 1872 for an alleged uprising became a turning point in national consciousness.

Influence on Rizal: these conditions gave him the raw material for Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo — novels that exposed friar abuse and colonial injustice — and pushed him toward reform through education rather than armed revolt.

Rizal's Family

Eleven children, one household of ideas

Father
Francisco Mercado Rizal

A prosperous farmer of Chinese-Filipino descent who leased land from the Dominican-owned hacienda in Calamba. Known for his discipline and strong sense of responsibility.

Mother
Teodora Alonso Realonda

A well-educated, well-read woman who personally taught young José to read and write, and introduced him to poetry — planting the seed for his literary gift.

Siblings — José was the seventh of eleven children
Saturnina Paciano Narcisa Olimpia Lucía María José Concepción Josefa Trinidad Soledad

Background & influence: the Rizals belonged to the principalía, the educated landholding class — comfortable but not immune to colonial abuse. Elder brother Paciano became José's mentor and financier, sending him to the Ateneo and later to Europe; the family's value for education and their firsthand experience of injustice (including their mother's wrongful imprisonment) directly shaped José's worldview.

Growing Up & Reaching Beyond the Islands

From Calamba to the capitals of Europe

1861 – 1871 · Calamba & Biñan

Childhood & First Lessons

Born June 19, 1861. His mother taught him the alphabet at age three and nurtured his love for reading and poetry; he later studied under a private tutor and then at a school in Biñan run by Justiniano Aquino Cruz.

  • Showed early gifts in poetry, drawing, and sculpture.
  • Grew up surrounded by books in a household that prized learning.
1871 – 1872 · A Turning Point

Injustice Close to Home

His mother was imprisoned on questionable charges, and the execution of the priests Gomburza in 1872 — to whom he would later dedicate El Filibusterismo — left a lasting mark on the young Rizal and his brother Paciano.

1872 – 1877 · Ateneo Municipal de Manila

Formal Education Begins

Enrolled at the Ateneo under the sponsorship of Paciano, graduating with the highest honors ("sobresaliente") at just 16 years old.

1877 – 1882 · University of Santo Tomas

Philosophy, Letters, and Medicine

Studied first Philosophy and Letters, then shifted to Medicine — but left after facing discrimination from Dominican professors, a key push toward studying abroad.

1882 – 1885 · Universidad Central de Madrid, Spain

Life Abroad Begins

Earned his Licentiate in Medicine and continued studies in Philosophy and Letters, immersing himself in liberal European thought and the Filipino expatriate community.

1885 – 1887 · Paris & Heidelberg

Specializing in Ophthalmology

Trained under Dr. Louis de Wecker in Paris and Dr. Otto Becker in Heidelberg, Germany — motivated partly by his mother's failing eyesight. It was in Germany that he completed and published his first novel.

  • 1887, Berlin: published Noli Me Tangere.
  • 1891, Ghent: published its sequel, El Filibusterismo.
1887 – 1892 · The Propaganda Movement

Achievements & Advocacy in Europe

Contributed to the reformist newspaper La Solidaridad, corresponded closely with Austrian scholar Ferdinand Blumentritt, and became a leading voice of the Propaganda Movement calling for reforms and Philippine representation in Spain.

1892 · Return to Manila

La Liga Filipina & Exile

Returned home to found La Liga Filipina, a civic association for peaceful reform. He was arrested soon after and exiled to Dapitan, where he continued to teach, practice medicine, and build community projects until his execution on December 30, 1896.

Reflection Question

Through written books, Rizal revealed the "social cancer" of his period, during an era when news was conveyed slowly because Church and State exercised strict authority over all thoughts. Many people believe that Rizal would choose modern digital channels to expose the troubles of the nation today, since contemporary information spreads without verification. Scholars question whether a novel would remain sufficient for Rizal when public attention moves quickly across instant messages.
